    Dates and times

    If the current major industrial powers were devastated in a hypothetical global war, the future linguistic landscape would depend less on pre-war prestige and more on which regions retained:

    • Population
    • Agricultural capacity
    • Industrial infrastructure
    • Political stability
    • Educational systems
    • Long-distance trade networks

    A few plausible outcomes:

    1. English remains dominant

    Even if the US, UK, and much of Europe were shattered, English has enormous momentum:

    • Widely taught as a second language.
    • Embedded in science, aviation, shipping, and computing.
    • Used throughout parts of Africa, South Asia, and Oceania.

    A post-collapse world might resemble the fate of Latin after Rome: the original centres gone, but the language surviving elsewhere.

    2. Hindi/Urdu becomes a major world language

    If the Indian subcontinent escaped comparatively intact:

    • Huge population base.
    • Existing scientific and industrial capabilities.
    • Long tradition of multilingual administration.

    Hindi, Urdu, or a simplified Hindustani could become a major trade language.

    3. Spanish expands dramatically

    Spanish has advantages:

    • Large population across the Americas.
    • Geographic dispersion.
    • Existing literary and educational infrastructure.

    If North America and Europe suffered badly while Latin America remained relatively functional, Spanish could become one of the principal international languages.

    4. Arabic gains influence

    If the Arabic-speaking world remained relatively stable:

    • Shared language across many states.
    • Important trade routes linking Africa, Asia, and Europe.
    • Strong religious and cultural institutions.

    Historically, Arabic became the lingua franca of a vast scientific and commercial sphere after the decline of earlier empires.

    5. Swahili becomes unexpectedly important

    For a longer-term (100–300 year) scenario:

    • East Africa has some of the world's fastest-growing populations.
    • Swahili already serves as a regional trade language.
    • It is not strongly tied to any one ethnic group.

    Many futurists underestimate how influential East Africa could become.

    6. Portuguese survives through Brazil

    If Brazil remained relatively intact:

    • Large population.
    • Agricultural self-sufficiency.
    • Significant industrial base.

    Portuguese could become much more important globally than it is today.

    7. New creoles and trade languages emerge

    History suggests the most likely outcome is not a single winner but hybridization.

    Examples:

    • Simplified international Englishes.
    • English-Hindi trade pidgins.
    • Arabic-Swahili commercial dialects.
    • Regional lingua francas replacing global ones.

    After the collapse of large empires, linguistic fragmentation is common. The period after the Western Roman Empire saw Latin survive, but local languages flourished.

    For a far-future SF setting

    A plausible ranking 200 years after such a catastrophe might be:

    1. English (simplified global version)
    2. Hindi/Hindustani
    3. Spanish
    4. Arabic
    5. Swahili
    6. Portuguese

    But the more interesting possibility is that none dominates completely. Instead, people might speak:

    • A local language at home.
    • A regional trade language.
    • A highly simplified global auxiliary descended from English, much as Medieval scholars used Latin.

    That pattern would actually be more consistent with most of human history than today's unusually globalized linguistic order.

    If the postwar founders are consciously trying to avoid English dominance, they might look for a term that exists across multiple major language families, or choose a word whose initial letter works well internationally.

    Here are some possibilities drawn from languages likely to remain influential in your scenario (Spanish, Hindi, Arabic, Swahili, Portuguese, Mandarin, etc.):

    A few combinations that sound plausible:

    KE

    Concord Enzi

    • Swahili-derived.
    • "Year 153 KE."

    KA

    Concord Aṣr

    • Arabic-derived.
    • Short and distinctive.

    KZ

    Concord Zaman

    • "314 KZ."
    • Has a Eurasian feel because zaman appears in Arabic, Persian, Turkish, Urdu, and related languages.

    KY

    Concord Yuga

    • Strong South Asian influence.
    • "Year 87 KY."
    • A yuga is traditionally a very long age, which may fit a civilization-founding event.

    KK

    Concord Kāla

    • "Year 421 KK."
    • Easy to remember.
    • Could become simply "the KK calendar."

    KJ

    Concord Ji

    • East Asian influence.
    • Very terse and bureaucratic.

    My favorites for a future global civilization are:

    KZ — Concord Zaman

    • Crosses Arabic, Persian, Turkish, Urdu cultural spheres.
    • Easy to pronounce almost anywhere.
    • Sounds like a technical standard: 214 KZ.

    KY — Concord Yuga

    • Evokes a new age of humanity rather than merely a new dating system.
    • Has a grand civilizational tone.

    KE — Concord Enzi

    • Swahili influence acknowledges the demographic and political rise of Africa.
    • Short, distinctive, and easy to read.

    For example:

    • Federation founded: 1 KZ
    • Current year: 184 KZ
    • Historians refer to "the Pre-Concord centuries."

    That has the same compact, institutional feel as UTC without obviously belonging to any present-day great power.

    Using KZ - if the contubuting cultures wanted to give UTC a new abbreviation what might it be

    That's an interesting worldbuilding problem because UTC itself is a compromise acronym:

    • English wanted CUT (Coordinated Universal Time).
    • French wanted TUC (Temps Universel Coordonné).
    • They settled on UTC, which belongs fully to neither language.

    A post-WWIII Concord civilization might deliberately repeat that process and choose a term that is recognizable but not owned by any participant culture.

    Suppose the major contributors are:

    • Hindustani sphere
    • Arabic sphere
    • Spanish/Portuguese sphere
    • Swahili sphere
    • Mandarin sphere
    • Remaining English speakers

    They might avoid "Universal" entirely because it sounds imperial, and instead emphasize Earth, Concord, or Planetary Coordination.

    Some plausible successors:

    KTC

    K (Concord) + T (Time) + C (Coordination)

    Nobody quite agrees what the letters mean.

    Official expansions might include:

    • Koncord Time Coordination
    • Tiempo Coordinado de la Concordia
    • Waqt al-Kunqurd al-Munassaq

    The abbreviation becomes its own thing.

    KZT

    Building directly on your calendar.

    KZ Time

    Like UTC today:

    • "14:32 KZT"
    • "Ship departed at 08:00 KZT."

    Officially it might mean:

    • Concord Zaman Time
    • Concord Zenith Time
    • Concord Zero Time

    Most people no longer know.

    KTC

    Koncord Terran Coordinate

    The "K" spelling is deliberate:

    • Not English.
    • Not Spanish.
    • Not Arabic.
    • Not Mandarin.
    • Everybody compromises.

    Exactly the sort of thing an international standards committee would invent.

    ZTK

    Derived from Zaman, Tiempo, Kāla.

    Each major linguistic bloc contributes one letter.

    Nobody gets first place.

    TAZ

    From:

    • Terran
    • Aṣr
    • Zaman

    A deliberately hybrid acronym.

    KTU

    The closest analogue to UTC.

    The participating cultures want a compromise nobody owns:

    • K = Concord
    • T = Terran/Time
    • U = Unified

    Official documents never fully define it.

    Like UTC, people eventually stop asking.

    My favorite for realism is KTU.

    The constitutional conference debates it for months. Nobody loves it. Everyone can tolerate it. A century later nobody remembers whether it originally stood for:

    Concord Time Unified

    or

    Terran Unified Concord

    or something else entirely.

    That's exactly how the most successful international standards often emerge.

    where might they place the prime meridian?

    If the old Euro-Atlantic order has been thoroughly discredited, Greenwich is unlikely to survive as the prime meridian. The choice would become intensely symbolic.

    There are several plausible approaches.

    1. Keep Greenwich anyway (the engineer's solution)

    Despite political objections, Greenwich has enormous practical advantages:

    • Every map, chart, satellite system, and historical record already references it.
    • Astronomical and geodetic systems are built around it.
    • The location itself becomes merely historical.

    The Concord might simply redefine it:

    "The Prime Meridian shall remain the established geodetic reference inherited from the Pre-Concord Era."

    Many people would grumble, but engineers would rejoice.

    Historically, this is probably the most likely outcome.

    2. Place it on the opposite side of Earth

    The anti-Greenwich solution:

    180° longitude

    Advantages:

    • Runs mostly through ocean.
    • Avoids giving prestige to any surviving state.
    • Symbolically rejects the old order.

    Maps would look odd to modern eyes but work perfectly well.

    3. A Pacific Meridian

    The postwar world may become economically centered on the Pacific and Indian Oceans rather than the Atlantic.

    A prime meridian could be placed through the central Pacific, perhaps near:

    • Hawaii
    • Kiribati
    • Empty ocean

    This emphasizes that Earth is a planet rather than a collection of continents.

    4. A Meridian through a New Capital

    Many federations do this historically.

    If the Concord creates a purpose-built capital, analogous to:

    • Brasília
    • Canberra
    • Washington, D.C.

    then longitude zero might run through the capital.

    Examples:

    • Concord City (East Africa)
    • New Alexandria (Indian Ocean)
    • Unity (purpose-built global capital)

    The downside is that future generations may resent the symbolism.

    5. The "Null Meridian"

    This is my favorite SF option.

    Instead of selecting a famous city, the Concord defines longitude zero by coordinates:

    Prime Meridian: 0K

    passing through a deliberately uninhabited site.

    Examples:

    • Mid-ocean island.
    • Antarctic research city.
    • Artificial island built specifically for the purpose.

    The location becomes sacred in a civic sense.

    Think of it as the equivalent of the meter bar in Paris, except for planetary geography.

    6. Astronomical Reference

    A technologically advanced Concord might define longitude by something entirely non-national.

    For example:

    Longitude 0 is the terrestrial meridian passing through the Earth-Moon barycentric reference vector at the founding instant of the Concord.

    This is exactly the sort of thing scientists would propose and politicians would hate.

    Eventually it would be simplified into maps and GPS receivers.

    For your KZ calendar

    A nice symbolic package would be:

    • Calendar: KZ (Concord Zaman)
    • Time standard: KTU
    • Prime Meridian: KM0 (Koncord Meridian Zero)

    located in the central Pacific (emphasizing a planetary rather than continental civilization).
